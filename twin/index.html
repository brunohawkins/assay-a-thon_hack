Cellular Perturb-seq on a chip

Grow different cell types side by side, let them change each other or keep them apart, and read every cell out by single-cell sequencing. Perturb-seq perturbs genes; this perturbs a cell's neighbours.

Present

    The idea

    Going from genotype to phenotype is the familiar direction: change a gene, see what the cell does. We want the other kind of experiment as well: change what a cell lives with (its neighbours, the medium they share, when they meet) and measure what that does to every cell.

    Today that means co-cultures in plates: hundreds of thousands of cells and millilitres of medium per condition, mixed populations that are hard to take apart afterwards, and only a few conditions at a time.

    What the platform does

    • Perturbs with cells, not genes. Each cell type grows in its own chamber. The flow decides whether they stay apart or one's medium reaches the others, and when.
    • Tests differentiation protocols and growth factors. Every chamber can have its own medium and schedule, side by side on one chip.
    • Reads out every cell. Our chips harvest, encapsulate, lyse and reverse-transcribe; then each capsule gets its own barcode in a bought droplet stage running our chemistry. One capsule holds one cell.
    • Knows where each cell came from. Before harvest, the cells of each chamber pick up that chamber's own label in Module 1, and the three cell types also separate by their transcriptomes.
    • Runs many conditions at once. The labels let conditions share one library and be told apart afterwards.

    Why small is the point

    A condition does not need a million cells. A few thousand cells talking to a few thousand cells is enough, in microlitre volumes where their signals stay concentrated, and with a fraction of the reagent.

    The yardstick is one well of a 10x Genomics chip; our pipeline barcodes the cells and builds the library itself. Today one Module 1 has three chambers, so three conditions per run and 24 across eight; the aim is ten or more chambers per chip and many chips in parallel. Targets from a mentor's estimate, not results.

    The use case, and beyond

    The first use case is the neural trio: neurons, microglia and astrocytes differentiated from iPSCs, and how each changes the others. Nothing ties the chip to these three. Any cell types that grow this way could be paired.

    What we built this weekend, and why

    Seven stages, culture to clean-up
    Five chips printed for the demo (Modules 1 to 5), a cell-prep chip designed but not printed, and a single-cell barcoding stage that runs our chemistry on a bought droplet kit; plus Module 6, a printed bulk-omics branch. Each chip is 30 × 20 mm and 0.3 mm deep with one fluidic layer, the envelope both resin printing and hot embossing can make.
    Shared file formats
    For protocols, chips, runs and the pipeline. Six people could build in parallel against placeholders, and every tool reads the same files.
    Prompt to chip
    A typed description becomes a chip spec built from Flowmatter library parts, and our checker tests it against the fabrication rules.
    The operator and its closed loop
    It plans, checks and corrects a run. With no hardware this weekend, it runs against a virtual chip with injected faults such as a clog.
    This digital twin
    One picture of the whole line for the team and for this demo: every chip, the sample moving through, and a bench view that runs beside the real chip.
    A dye demo for every printed chip
    One syringe pump and food dye in water, with each chip's feed, vent and pump rate derived from its spec. The twin runs beside it on one clock: if the dye arrives when the twin says it will, the model has earned some trust.

    What is new

    • Routing by flow, not valves. Module 1 grows neurons, microglia and astrocytes in three chambers that meet at one junction. Which ports are fed, open or plugged decides whether the cultures stay apart or share medium.
    • Single-cell barcodes made in droplets. After reverse transcription each capsule goes into its own oil droplet, where one random barcode is amplified and joined onto that capsule's cDNA. One capsule holds one cell, so each cell gets its own barcode.
    • The AI does not get the last word. The operator suggests, the flow model predicts, and deterministic rules decide. Asked to do something that would mix the cultures, it refuses and says why.
    • One description drives every tool. The protocol and the chip spec feed the CAD, the operator and the twin, so the picture cannot drift away from the design.
    • The model and the bench on one clock. The twin predicts when the dye reaches each chamber; the bench demo records when it actually does.

    Where it goes next

    • Scale out. More chambers per culture chip, and more chips running in parallel.
    • Connect cultures on demand. Grow each culture on its own chip and link them only when they should meet. Membrane valves (a thin flexible membrane between two channels, opened and closed by pressure) would make that one self-contained system with no tubing.
    • Contact, not just medium. Microchannels too narrow for cells but wide enough for axons and the long processes of microglia and astrocytes, for co-culture by cell-to-cell contact.

    Where it stands

    • Everything on screen is a simulation. There are no pumps, cameras or wet-lab data this weekend, and the closed loop runs on a virtual chip.
    • Six chips are being printed for the demo: Modules 1 to 6 (Module 2 is the encapsulation chip). Their geometry is frozen. Cell prep and the barcoding layout are not printed; the barcoding stage runs on a bought kit, because its features are below the in-plane floor. Every demo-scale chip has a dye-demo procedure.
    • It is a single-cell assay: the per-capsule barcode resolves cells. The lane label from Module 1 and the four sublibrary indices from Module 4 only help sort the data.
    • Feeding is the open problem at demo scale: the demo Module 1 schedule gives too little medium and caps the chambers for a day, about seven times longer than the oxygen in a sealed chamber lasts. The full-scale Module 1 design perfuses continuously, sized by the cells' oxygen demand, with gas exchange in a vented reservoir per lane, and still stays far below the shear limit for adherent cells.
    • A bulk metabolome and proteome arm (Module 6) branches off culture. It needs a sub-ambient stage the rig does not have yet.
    • Capsule retention cannot be printed in-plane at this size: it uses a bought mesh insert, or a shallow weir under the lid.
